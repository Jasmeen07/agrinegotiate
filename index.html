Fair prices on a bad signal

A small farmer often negotiates blind: no market data, a phone that drops out, and a buyer who knows the going rate. We trained software agents to bargain on the farmer's behalf, then measured whether the deals came out fair.

Two sides have to agree on a price, and each keeps its own limit secret. We gave each side a small neural network and let them negotiate thousands of times in a simulated market. Then we asked: do they reach a deal, is the split fair, and what happens when the phone line drops?

In short

The agents learn to trade efficiently, but the farmer usually ends up with about a quarter of the gain instead of half. Perishable crops and competition between farmers make that worse.

An adaptive fee on unfair deals helps the farmer, and with longer training it clears its target and beats a price floor on real prices. A weak signal hurts badly, and giving agents a memory did not fix it. Three of our five hypotheses failed in the first runs, and we report them as they came out.

1The problem

A farmer has a sack of tomatoes that will not keep. The buyer in the town market knows today's price. The farmer, on a phone that works half the time, does not.

Every farmer has a fallback: the price they could get elsewhere, or what the crop is worth after it spoils. Every buyer has a value: the most the goods are worth to them. Any price between the two leaves both better off. The question is where in that gap the price lands.Economists call the size of that gap the gains from trade. We call it the gain.

People settle this with experience, pressure and inside information, so a farmer without them starts behind. If agents can learn to negotiate well, they could level that out. We built the benchmark first, before training anything (section 5).

2How a negotiation works

One farmer and one buyer take turns for at most 30 steps. On each turn a side can do one of four things.

Offer a price
One of 21 prices on a grid from 0.20 to 1.20. The offer stays open for six steps.
Accept
Take the other side's open offer. The deal is done at that price.
Walk away
End the talks and take the fallback.
Wait
Do nothing this turn. A dropped signal often forces this.

The farmer sees only their own fallback and the buyer's offers; the buyer sees only their own value and the farmer's offers. That hidden information is what makes it a negotiation.

A worked example

Say the farmer's fallback is 800 rupees a quintal and the buyer's value is 1,300. The gain is 500. A price of 1,050 splits it evenly (the Nash price). A price of 930 gives the farmer 130 of the 500, a 26 percent share, close to what our trained agents settle on.

What a bad signal does

When the link is down, the farmer can neither send nor read messages. An offer can expire unseen, and an acceptance can arrive too late.The link switches on and off at random, with outages averaging two steps. We vary how much of the time it is up.

4How the agents learn

Each side is a small neural network. It reads 22 numbers, picks one of 24 actions, and is rewarded only by the gain it ends up with. Nobody tells it how to bargain.

What an agent sees and can do

It sees 22 numbers
Time left, its deadline, its own walk-away value, the other side's latest price and how old it is, its own open offer and whether it was delivered, whether the link is up, and how many talks are open. The buyer also sees the fee rate and target.
It chooses 1 of 24
Twenty-one prices, accept, walk away or wait. A mask blocks illegal moves, such as accepting when no offer is open.
It is rewarded by
Its own gain when the talks end, minus a small cost per step of delay.

The learning rule

We use independent PPO: the farmer and buyer networks each learn from their own experience while the other keeps changing. In every batch, 30 percent of episodes pair a learner with a scripted opponent, so the two networks do not just learn to exploit each other's habits.Learning rate 3e-4 falling linearly, clip 0.2, GAE 0.95, four epochs of four minibatches, entropy bonus 0.01 to 0.001, 64 hidden units.

Putting fairness into the loss

When a deal lands below a fair target price, the buyer pays a fee. The fee rate, lambda, is not set by hand: it rises while buyers keep pushing prices too low and eases off when they stop (dual ascent, a standard method for constrained reinforcement learning).

fee = lambda x max(0, target price - deal price)
target price = farmer fallback + 0.4 x gain
buyer advantage = reward advantage - lambda x cost advantage
lambda ← clip( lambda + eta x (average shortfall - 0.02), 0, 5 ), eta = 0.05

5How we measured

There is no public benchmark for farmer and buyer negotiation, so we built the comparison set ourselves. Every group is scored with the same metrics on the same fixed evaluation episodes.

The four main numbers

Efficiency
How much of the available gain the two sides captured together. One means none was wasted.
Farmer share
The farmer's part of the captured gain. We call 0.5 fair.
Agreement rate
How often a pair reached a deal at all.
Nash distance
How far the price landed from the even split.
efficiency = (farmer gain + buyer gain) / largest possible gain
farmer share = farmer gain / (farmer gain + buyer gain)

We also track the worst-off tenth of farmers, shortfall against the fee target, fees paid, price inequality across farmers, spoilage, unsold farmers and time to agreement.

How sure we are

Each setting is trained several times from different random starts. We plot each run as a dot, mark the interquartile mean, and draw a 95 percent bootstrap interval. A hypothesis passes only if the whole interval clears its threshold. With three to five runs the interval is wide, so we label those results exploratory.The interquartile mean drops the best and worst quarter of runs, so one lucky or failed run cannot move it much.

6What we found

We wrote down five hypotheses and their success thresholds before running the experiments. In our first, shorter runs one held, one held in part, and three failed. We then reran the main comparisons at the planned 3 million steps per run: the fee result moved from mostly failed to held in part, and the other rerun results kept their verdicts.

FailedH1 · 10 runs

Equal conditions do not give equal splits

On equal footing the learners trade efficiently (efficiency 0.84, interval 0.83 to 0.86), but the farmer's share is nowhere near one half. In eight of ten runs it settles around 0.26, and in two it sits near 0.65. Longer training did not fix this: at 3 million steps efficiency falls to 0.62 (interval 0.60 to 0.67), nine runs settle near 0.24 and one at 0.87.

In the equal-conditions row, the dots form two clusters, not one.

Held for two of fourH2 · 5 to 10 runs

Unequal conditions hurt the farmer, in two ways

A perishable crop cuts the farmer's share to 0.16 (interval 0.14 to 0.18, ten runs). Three farmers competing for one buyer cuts it to 0.14 (five runs). An earlier farmer deadline and a noisy price signal made no clear difference. Both clear effects hold at 3 million steps: the perishable-crop share is 0.19 and the three-farmer share 0.10.

Held for two of three at 3M stepsH3 · 3 to 10 runs

The fee helps, and with longer training it catches up with the floor

Our main result. In the perishable-crop market, an adaptive fee raises the farmer's share from 0.16 to 0.27 and lifts efficiency from 0.67 to 0.72. In these first runs it still misses our 0.40 target, and a plain price floor does better on both: efficiency of about 0.83 and a farmer share of 0.39 to 0.54, depending on where the floor sits.

At 3 million steps the fee does much better: the farmer share reaches 0.40, efficiency 0.82, and the farmer's gain rises by 0.19 (interval 0.15 to 0.21), clearing our 0.10 target. It now matches the floor at 0.55, but the floor at 0.60 still gives the farmer more, so the fee still does not beat every floor.

Compare the gold fee rows with the green floor rows. The charts show the first, shorter runs.

When buyers differ a lot, the floor's lead disappears

In the main market every buyer values the crop about the same, so a floor rarely blocks a trade. In a market where buyer values vary widely (ten runs per setting), floors lose deals as they tighten: the deal rate falls from 0.55 to 0.41 to 0.24. The fee keeps trading at 0.66, but two of its ten runs collapsed to no trade. At a farmer share of about 0.30, the fee and the lightest floor reach the same efficiency, around 0.57. At 3 million steps (five runs per setting) the fee pulls ahead: efficiency 0.70 and farmer share 0.42, against 0.59 and 0.28 for the lightest floor, though one of the five fee runs still collapsed.

Checked against real prices

We used daily lemon prices from Agmarknet, India's government mandi price records, to set the market's price level, how stale a farmer's price signal is, and how much fallback prices differ. The bargaining is still simulated. In the first runs the fee lifts the farmer's gain by 0.09 (interval 0.06 to 0.11) without costing efficiency, and a floor again does better, though with only five runs. At 3 million steps this turns around: the fee lifts the farmer's gain by 0.27 (interval 0.20 to 0.30) and reaches efficiency 0.83 and a farmer share of 0.53, ahead of the floor's 0.82 and 0.37.

HeldH4 · 3 runs per point

A weak signal destroys trade

With the link always up, efficiency is 0.65. At 60 percent uptime it drops to 0.36. At 40 percent it is zero: every run stopped trading.

FailedH5 · 3 runs per point

Delay-aware agents did not help

Agents with a memory and a view of the link state reached 0.38 at 60 percent uptime, against 0.36 without, and the interval of the difference includes zero. On the real-price rural market the result was the same (0.35 aware, 0.40 unaware, five runs each).

The aware and unaware dots overlap at every uptime level.

Budget check76 runs · 3 million steps each

Training for longer helps the fee and hurts the equal market

Our first runs used 0.3 to 0.7 million steps, short of the 3 million we planned. We reran the main comparisons at 3 million steps with the same settings and seeds. Six of the nine rerun checks now pass, against four before. The fee gained the most. Learners in the equal market got less efficient, and the unfair markets still hurt the farmer.

7Where this goes next

Each setting was first trained three to ten times for 0.3 to 0.7 million steps, and the main comparisons were then repeated at 3 million steps (three to ten runs each). The delay study and the deadline and noisy-signal markets were not rerun. The fairness rules were tested in the one-farmer, one-buyer perishable market, the delay study in a rural one-to-one market, and the eight-farmer markets only with scripted agents.
